# 18 — Interview, Capstone and Career

**This chapter is not a read. It is a working manual.**

Chapters 01–17 taught the system. This one converts that into performance:
what you say, how you say it, and what you do when you do not know.

**How to use it.** Do not read it end to end and feel prepared — that feeling is
the trap. Work through it out loud, with a timer, preferably to another person
or a recording. **Reading this chapter is worth about an hour. Doing it is worth
about four, plus the capstone.**

**Everything here supports one of four goals:**

1. Explain the project clearly *(Parts B, D)*
2. Defend engineering decisions *(Parts C, E)*
3. Answer questions naturally *(Parts F, G, H)*
4. Find your remaining weaknesses and close them *(Parts I, J, K)*

---

# Part A — Three Rules Before Anything Else

**Rule 1 — Never claim what you cannot show.** This project is not deployed and
has no users. Saying "serving thousands of users" invites one follow-up
question that ends the interview's goodwill. **You have something better than
scale: depth.** Almost nobody at your level can explain why rank fusion uses
positions instead of scores, or why a health check probing the wrong port
stopped two services from starting.

**Rule 2 — Lead with the decision, not the technology.** "We used Redis" is a
fact. "We needed slow work off the request path, and Redis was already there for
caching, so a separate broker would have been machinery with no benefit" is an
engineer talking. **Every answer in this chapter follows: problem → choice →
alternatives → trade-off.**

**Rule 3 — Volunteer the weakness.** When you say "we don't have backups yet,
and that is the first thing I would add because it is the only failure we could
not recover from", you have demonstrated judgment that a polished answer cannot.
**Interviewers trust people who tell them the bad news first.**

# Part B — The Project Story, Four Lengths

Learn all four. Which one you use depends on who asked and why.

## B.1 Thirty seconds — for "tell me about a project"

> "It's a document question-answering system. You upload contracts or reports,
> ask a question in plain language, and get an answer that cites the exact page
> it came from — and refuses when your documents don't contain the answer.
>
> The interesting part isn't the chat box, it's making the answer checkable. The
> backend is FastAPI with PostgreSQL and a background worker; the retrieval
> combines keyword and semantic search, reranks the results, and the citations
> are written by code rather than generated by the model."

**Practise until it is 30 seconds, not 50.** The last sentence is the hook — it
invites the follow-up you want.

## B.2 Two minutes — for "walk me through your project"

> "The problem is that people have documents they can't search properly.
> Ctrl-F only finds exact words — if a contract says 'the lessee shall vacate'
> and you search 'when do I move out', you get nothing.
>
> So: you upload documents, and a background worker extracts the text — with OCR
> for scans — cuts it into chunks of about 1,800 characters on paragraph
> boundaries, and turns each chunk into a list of 1,024 numbers representing its
> meaning. Those go into PostgreSQL with the pgvector extension.
>
> When you ask a question, we run two searches: one by meaning using those
> vectors, one by keyword using Postgres full-text search. They fail in
> different ways — embeddings miss clause numbers and names, keywords miss
> paraphrase — so we merge them by rank rather than by score, because the two
> scores aren't on comparable scales. Then a cross-encoder re-reads the top
> thirty against the question and picks the best few.
>
> Those get wrapped in evidence blocks labelled with the real filename and page,
> which is why citations are trustworthy — the model copies a label rather than
> inventing one. Then Gemini writes the answer and we stream it token by token
> over server-sent events.
>
> Architecturally it's a FastAPI app, a Celery worker, and a scheduler — three
> processes from one Docker image — with Redis as the queue and cache, and
> PostgreSQL for everything else."

**Time it. Two minutes is roughly 300 words.** If you run over, cut the
ingestion detail — the interviewer will ask if they care.

## B.3 Five minutes — the technical walkthrough

Structure it as **four movements**, and say the movement names aloud so the
listener can follow:

**1. Ingestion (60s).** Upload is three calls — ask where to put it, upload the
bytes, confirm — so a 40 MB file never passes through the API. Confirming writes
a row and queues a job; the request returns in milliseconds. A Celery worker
does extraction, OCR, chunking, embedding, batched commits, and flips the status
to READY. Only READY documents are searchable.

**2. Retrieval (90s).** Embed the question; two searches, thirty candidates
each; Reciprocal Rank Fusion by position; deduplicate; cross-encoder rerank;
sort survivors back into document order; pack into a 6,000-token budget as
labelled evidence blocks.

**3. Generation (60s).** Prompt = injection guard + coverage rules + citation
format + exact refusal sentence + evidence. Temperature 0.2 because this is not
creative writing. Stream tokens as SSE, with sources sent before the first word.

**4. Production (90s).** One Docker image, three processes. Config entirely from
environment variables, ten of them required with no defaults so a missing secret
crashes at boot. Migrations run before the server starts. Health checks test the
database and Redis. Structured JSON logs with a correlation id and personal data
redacted. Then name two gaps: graceful shutdown and backups.

**Ending on the gaps is deliberate.** It converts "he described his project"
into "he has judgment about it".

## B.4 The deep version — when they keep asking

Do not prepare a monologue for this. Prepare **five threads you can pull**, and
let them choose:

| Thread | Opening line |
|---|---|
| Tenancy | "The most instructive bug was that we had two ID columns and only one was a tenant key…" |
| Concurrency | "We had three separate incidents where blocking code froze the whole server…" |
| Retrieval | "The interesting question is why you can't just add the two scores together…" |
| Trust | "We shipped a confidence score that turned out to be mostly hardcoded…" |
| Deployment | "The connection budget is fifteen, and that number decides more than you'd think…" |

**Each thread is a story you already know from Part E.** Having five means you
never repeat yourself and never run out.

# Part C — The Decision Defence Deck

Sixteen decisions, each in the same shape. **Rehearse the compressed version;
the full version is your safety net for follow-ups.**

**The template for every answer:** *problem → what we chose → what else we
considered → the trade-off we accepted → when I'd choose differently.*

### C.1 Why FastAPI, not Flask or Django?

> "About 86% of a request here is *waiting* — for the database and for the model
> API. FastAPI is async-native, so one worker serves many concurrent waiting
> requests. Answers also stream token by token, which ASGI expresses naturally
> and WSGI doesn't. And with 24 endpoint modules, validation and docs generated
> from type hints save real maintenance.
>
> Django would have given me an admin panel, an ORM and auth in an afternoon —
> I had to choose and wire all of those separately. If this were a content site
> with an admin interface, Django would be the better answer."

### C.2 Why PostgreSQL, not MongoDB?

> "The data is relational — users own documents, documents have pages, pages
> have chunks — and I need those links enforced, plus transactions so a
> half-processed document doesn't leave orphan rows.
>
> The deciding factor was pgvector: I needed similarity search over embeddings,
> and Postgres can do that with an extension. That let me keep one database.
> MongoDB would have suited a document-shaped schema with no joins, which isn't
> what this is."

### C.3 Why pgvector, not Pinecone or Qdrant?

> "Arithmetic. Each embedding is 1,024 numbers at 4 bytes — 4 KB. A 100-page
> document is about 200 chunks, so 1.2 MB. A thousand users with twenty
> documents each is roughly 24 GB, which fits one Postgres instance
> comfortably.
>
> A second store means a second thing to back up, secure and keep consistent —
> and it breaks transactional deletes, so 'delete this document and its chunks'
> stops being one operation. The seam exists if we outgrow it: retrieval goes
> through one function, and there's a `VECTOR_BACKEND` setting."

### C.4 Why Redis?

> "Two jobs. It's the message broker between the API and the worker, and it's
> the retrieval cache with a five-minute TTL.
>
> It was already needed for one, so a dedicated broker like RabbitMQ would be
> extra machinery for no gain at this scale. The honest trade-off is that Redis
> isn't durable by default — if it dies with jobs in it, they're gone. That's
> survivable here because the document row still says PROCESSING and can be
> requeued. It would *not* be acceptable for payments, and there I'd want a
> broker that writes to disk before acknowledging."

### C.5 Why Celery / background workers?

> "Processing a 200-page scan takes minutes. In a request that breaks four
> things: proxies close connections that produce nothing for a minute; it holds
> one of about fifteen database connections the whole time; the user has no
> progress and refreshes, starting a duplicate; and a deploy destroys the work
> with no record.
>
> So the request writes a row, queues a job and returns in milliseconds. The
> side benefit is scaling processing capacity without adding web servers."

### C.6 Why hybrid retrieval?

> "Because the two methods fail in different, non-overlapping ways. Embeddings
> miss exact identifiers — a clause number carries almost no meaning, so it gets
> squashed — plus names, numbers and negation. Keyword search misses paraphrase
> entirely. Each failure is *categorical*, so a whole class of question breaks
> every time with either alone."

### C.7 Why RRF instead of adding the scores?

> "The scores aren't comparable. Cosine similarity is roughly 0 to 1 with a
> meaningful zero; the lexical rank is a small unbounded number that depends on
> the corpus. Adding them means one silently dominates, and the balance shifts
> as data changes.
>
> RRF sums 1/(60 + rank) instead. Rank 1 means the same thing in both lists
> whatever the scale. It also rewards agreement — a chunk ranked 3rd and 4th
> beats one ranked 1st in a single list — which is right, because two methods
> that fail differently agreeing is strong evidence."

### C.8 Why rerank if you already have scores?

> "The first-stage scores come from a bi-encoder, which embedded each chunk
> without knowing the question — fast, because it's precomputed, but the chunk's
> embedding has to serve every possible question. A cross-encoder reads the
> question and chunk together and is far more accurate, and far too slow to run
> over a corpus.
>
> So: cheap and wide to thirty, expensive and narrow to five. We only send five
> to the model, and sending the wrong five gives a wrong answer even when the
> right text was retrieved."

### C.9 Why local embeddings but a hosted LLM?

> "Cost shape. Embedding runs hundreds of times per document at ingestion — a
> per-call price would scale with the corpus, so running bge-m3 on our own
> machine makes it free after the hardware. It's also multilingual, which
> matters for Indian-language documents.
>
> Generation happens once per question and needs a much larger model than we
> could host, so that's hosted. The rule I'd generalise: run the high-volume
> small model yourself, buy the low-volume large one."

### C.10 Why SSE, not WebSockets?

> "Data flows one way — server to browser. WebSockets add a separate protocol,
> a different authentication story because cookies behave differently on the
> handshake, reconnection logic and proxy configuration, all to solve a two-way
> problem we don't have.
>
> SSE is plain HTTP: same cookie, same CSRF check, same middleware. The cost is
> the browser can't send anything back on that connection, so cancellation uses
> a separate mechanism — and some proxies buffer SSE and must be configured not
> to."

### C.11 Why JWT in a cookie, not sessions or localStorage?

> "Sessions need a database lookup per request, and our whole deployment has
> about fifteen connections — that's the scarcest resource, so spending it on
> something arithmetic can do for free is wrong.
>
> The cookie is `HttpOnly`, so JavaScript can't read it; anything in
> localStorage is readable by any script, and one XSS bug becomes total account
> theft. The trade-off is that cookies are sent automatically, which is what
> makes CSRF possible — so `SameSite=strict` plus a double-submit token.
>
> The honest cost of tokens: I can't revoke instantly. Bounded by a 60-minute
> expiry; I'd add a revocation list checked at refresh."

### C.12 Why Docker?

> "Development is on Windows, deployment is Linux, and several Python packages
> here wrap C libraries — the Postgres driver, the OCR engine, image
> processing. Copying files means the OS, library versions and system packages
> all differ, and the errors mention Python while not being about Python.
>
> With an image, what I tested is byte-for-byte what runs — and rollback is
> running the previous image, which still exists."

### C.13 Why Compose, and why not Kubernetes?

> "Compose runs six containers on one machine with one command, which is exactly
> the development problem. It doesn't do multi-machine scheduling or rolling
> updates — that's where Kubernetes fits.
>
> For three processes and one developer, Kubernetes would be more complex than
> the thing it manages. The sequence I'd follow is Compose for development, a
> managed platform for small production, Kubernetes when you have enough
> services and people that its coordination pays for its cost."

### C.14 Why CI?

> "Because 'it works on my machine' is a statement about leftover packages and
> forgotten environment variables. CI is a fresh machine that installs
> everything from scratch — and the *emptiness* is the value.
>
> It runs against a real Postgres with pgvector and a real Redis, runs the
> migrations — which tests the migrations, not just the code — then the tests,
> then a frontend production build. The dependency audit is blocking, because a
> check whose failure is ignored isn't a check."

### C.15 Why the extract-then-compute rule?

> "The model extracts values; Python does every calculation. Language models are
> unreliable at arithmetic and confident about it, and there's no way to see it
> happened. Splitting the task on that line makes a whole class of error
> impossible rather than unlikely — and it means prompt injection can't change a
> financial ratio, because the model never computes one."

### C.16 Why monitoring, and what's missing?

> "Three signals answering different questions: structured JSON logs with a
> correlation id for one request, Prometheus metrics for aggregate rate, errors
> and latency, and traces that follow a request from the API into the worker so
> one trace shows an 80-millisecond request that queued a four-minute job.
>
> What's missing is alerting. We have all three signals and nothing watches
> them, which means users are the monitoring. Two of our real incidents were
> visible only as a queue growing to 134 jobs that nobody was looking at."

# Part E — Incident Stories

Eight stories, rehearsed. Each is roughly 45 seconds spoken. **Format: symptom →
cause → why it hid → fix → lesson.**

These are the single highest-value thing in your preparation, because "tell me
about a bug you fixed" is asked in almost every interview and most candidates
have nothing specific.

### E.1 The tenancy leak

> "We had two ID columns that looked alike: `owner_id`, the real tenant key, and
> `workspace_id`, which is derived from a workspace name — so it's identical for
> every user in the system. Several queries filtered on the second alone, which
> looked like isolation and provided none. One of them meant a chat could be
> shared as a public link readable with no login.
>
> It hid because every test seeded one account, and with one tenant a correct
> filter and no filter return the same rows.
>
> We fixed it at the session layer — models inherit a marker and a hook injects
> the owner filter into every ORM read, failing closed if no scope is set — so a
> new endpoint can't forget a filter it never writes. And we verified with two
> real accounts, negative *and* positive controls, because a 404 alone proves
> nothing if the URL is just wrong."

### E.2 The blocked event loop

> "Unrelated endpoints, including the health check, got slow whenever anyone
> streamed an answer. The AI provider's own latency was normal.
>
> The cause: the streaming response is a blocking generator, and we were
> iterating it on the event loop. The call that *obtained* the stream was
> correctly offloaded to a thread — the iteration that did the network I/O
> wasn't. It looked correct, which is why review missed it.
>
> The fix steps the iterator through a thread one item at a time, with a timeout
> per chunk rather than per stream, because a long answer is legitimate and a
> chunk that never arrives isn't. The test runs a heartbeat coroutine while
> consuming a blocking stream — the broken version recorded zero heartbeats over
> 0.6 seconds. That's the defect as a number, not a proxy for it."

### E.3 The unregistered tasks

> "Four workspaces — Legal, Finance, Study, Research — had processing tasks that
> were routed and dispatched and never imported by the worker. So messages
> arrived with a name the worker didn't know and were rejected. The endpoint had
> already returned success, because `.delay()` only writes a message.
>
> Bulk résumé ingest in HR was similar — it had never once stored a document
> where HR could find it.
>
> That's where our three-way rule came from: registered, routed, *and* consumed
> by a running worker. Three files have to agree and nothing checks that they
> do, so the real fix is a startup assertion that refuses to boot otherwise."

### E.4 The cache that never invalidated

> "You could delete a document, see it disappear, ask the same question, and
> still get an answer built from its text with page citations — for up to five
> minutes.
>
> The write path stored keys as `retrieval:{workspace}:{hash}` and the delete
> path purged `retrieval:uid_{user}:*`. They never matched. Nothing errored,
> because deleting zero keys is a successful operation and looks exactly like an
> empty cache.
>
> The fix is one function that builds the key with the prefix constraint in its
> docstring. The stronger version, which I'd still do, derives the purge pattern
> from that same function so drift becomes impossible rather than documented."

### E.5 The silent no-op

> "Six places imported a Redis library inside a try/except that returned None on
> failure. The library wasn't installed, so every call failed — and because
> callers treat None as 'no cache entry', a missing dependency was
> indistinguishable from a cache miss.
>
> That silently disabled three things: the retrieval cache, so every query paid
> full cost while the code *looked* like it had a cache; an advertised abuse
> control that stopped repeat trial registrations; and two rate limits, which
> failed open.
>
> The repair was to use the library that's actually installed and log at ERROR
> with the exception type, once per process. Degrading is fine; degrading
> silently is what turned one missing package into three dead features."

### E.6 The health check that stopped two services

> "PgBouncer's image defaults to listening on 5432, while our port mapping, the
> health check and the application all targeted 6432. So the check probed a
> closed port and the container was unhealthy forever — and because the worker
> and scheduler declared `depends_on: service_healthy`, they never started at
> all.
>
> What I like about this one is that nothing was broken *except* the
> configuration. The health check reported a real failure and `depends_on` did
> exactly what it was told. A visible failure is the system working."

### E.7 The connection budget

> "Our hosted database allows about fifteen connections for the whole project.
> Pool sizes were hardcoded in the session module at ten plus twenty overflow —
> thirty for the API alone.
>
> Measured with the worker stopped, the API held all fifteen at rest, fourteen
> idle. The worker couldn't drain its queue and the health probe couldn't open a
> sixteenth connection, so the container sat unhealthy for nineteen hours.
>
> The fix moved the numbers into configuration with the arithmetic written next
> to them — API five, worker two children at two each, scheduler two, health
> ping one: twelve of fifteen. The lesson is that anything with a deployment
> ceiling is configuration, not code."

### E.8 The metric that was a constant

> "We shipped a trust score — five weighted factors, shown as HIGH, MEDIUM or
> LOW next to every answer. An audit found three of the five were hardcoded
> constants, about 65% of the weight, and a fourth compared the first fifty
> characters of a chunk verbatim against the model's prose, which never matches
> because models paraphrase. It returned roughly 66/MEDIUM for everything — and
> was re-exported into a compliance PDF claiming it indicated retrieval
> confidence.
>
> It survived because a constant can never contradict an answer, so nothing
> triggered a review.
>
> The rule I took from it: a number displayed to a user needs a test proving it
> varies with its inputs. Feed a well-supported answer and a contradicted one
> and assert the scores differ."

# Part F — Question Banks

Answer each aloud before reading the model answer. **The model answers are
deliberately short** — an interview answer of more than 90 seconds without a
follow-up is a monologue.

## F.1 HR and behavioural

**"Tell me about yourself."**
> "I'm a final-year student who's spent the last several months building a
> production-grade RAG system — document upload, hybrid retrieval, cited
> answers, streaming, background workers, the whole deployment path. What I got
> out of it wasn't the feature list, it was learning to find my own defects: we
> ran a line-by-line audit that produced 83 findings, and fixing them taught me
> more than building it did. I'm looking for a backend or AI engineering role
> where that kind of rigour matters."

*Mistake:* reciting your CV chronologically. *Follow-up:* "What was the hardest
finding?" → Part E.

**"Tell me about a time you failed."**
> "I introduced a regression. I fixed the upload path so documents stored the
> workspace they were actually uploaded into — correct fix — but I didn't check
> what *read* that value. Five endpoints then 404'd for six of seven
> workspaces, including delete, so those documents couldn't be removed by
> anyone.
>
> The rule I wrote down afterwards is: when you change how a value is written,
> enumerate its readers in the same change. It's now part of how we review."

*Why this works:* a real failure, your own, with a durable lesson.

**"Why should we hire you?"** → Part H.9.

## F.2 Python and concurrency

**"What is the GIL and does it matter here?"**
> "Only one thread runs Python bytecode at a time. It matters twice. It's why
> our background worker uses separate processes rather than threads. And it's
> why offloading model inference to a thread still works — the libraries release
> the GIL while computing in C, so it genuinely runs in parallel."

**"async versus threads versus processes?"**
> "Async for waiting — one thread, many concurrent requests, cheap because a
> suspended coroutine is a few hundred bytes. Threads for blocking work you
> can't avoid, so it doesn't hold the loop. Processes for CPU-bound Python that
> needs real parallelism, or for work that must survive independently. We use
> all three: async request path, threads for embedding and reranking, processes
> for the worker."

**"What does `await` actually do?"**
> "Suspends this coroutine and hands control back to the event loop, which runs
> something else until the awaited thing is ready. It doesn't create a thread,
> doesn't make anything faster, and doesn't run anything in the background. It
> makes waiting free."

*Common mistake:* saying async makes code faster. *Follow-up:* "So what happens
if I do heavy computation in an async function?" → everyone waits; two fixes.

**"Mutable default arguments?"**
> "The default is created once at definition, not per call, so
> `def f(x, acc=[])` shares one list across every call that omits it. Use `None`
> and create inside — and check `is None`, not truthiness, so a legitimately
> empty list the caller passed is respected."

## F.3 FastAPI and backend

**"Walk me through a request."** → Chapter 13's seventeen steps, compressed to
five: Uvicorn parses HTTP → middleware chain → route match → dependencies
resolve, which is where auth and the DB session happen → your function →
serialise → back out through the middleware.

**"Explain dependency injection."**
> "A function declares what it needs instead of fetching it, and the framework
> provides it. There's no magic — it reads the signature, sees the marker, calls
> the function first. Value: no repetition across two hundred endpoints, and
> requirements are visible in the signature so a reviewer can see which
> endpoints are protected. Our DB dependency uses `yield`, so the code after the
> yield returns the connection to the pool when the request ends."

**"Middleware order?"**
> "`add_middleware` wraps the app, so the last added runs first. It matters:
> a layer that rejects requests placed inside a logging layer means rejected
> requests are never logged, so attacks are invisible."

**"How do you validate input?"**
> "Pydantic models at the boundary. FastAPI parses and validates before my
> function runs and returns 422 naming the field. It's a single choke point —
> by the time my code starts, every field is present and correctly typed, so
> there's no defensive checking and none can be forgotten."

## F.4 Databases and SQL

**"What is an index and what does it cost?"**
> "A structure that finds matching rows without reading them all — a B-tree
> turns a million-row scan into three or four levels. The cost is on writes:
> every insert and update maintains every index, and an unused index is pure
> loss. Index what you filter, join or sort by."

**"Explain ACID."** → four promises, one line each, then: "The one worth
discussing is isolation, because it has levels — Postgres defaults to Read
Committed, where each statement sees a fresh snapshot, which is exactly why a
check in application code followed by a separate update is a race."

**"Tell me about a concurrency bug in a database."** → the trial-limit TOCTOU:
two requests at 9 both pass the check, both increment, user gets 11 on a
ten-query trial. Fix: `UPDATE … SET used = used + 1 WHERE id = ? AND used <
:limit`, zero rows affected means refuse.

**"How do you change a schema safely?"**
> "Migrations, and additive changes only during a rolling deploy, because old
> and new code run together. Adding a nullable column is safe; renaming isn't —
> autogenerate turns it into a drop plus an add, which destroys data. A rename
> is three deploys: add nullable and write both, backfill and switch reads, then
> drop."

**"What's an N+1?"** → fetch N things, then query per item; 51 queries instead
of 2, sequential round trips. Fixes: `selectinload`, `joinedload`, manual
`.in_()`, or eager on the relationship. Then: "Ours was in an export and a
request-path endpoint — and fixing it uncovered a cross-tenant read in the same
WHERE clause."

## F.5 AI and RAG

**"What is RAG and why not a bigger context window?"** → C.6-style; five
reasons, only one about size.

**"How do embeddings work?"**
> "A model turns text into 1,024 numbers arranged so similar meanings land near
> each other. Trained on huge numbers of text pairs until matching ones land
> close. We compare with cosine similarity, which uses direction rather than
> length, because length encodes how long or emphatic the text is rather than
> what it's about."

**"What causes hallucination?"**
> "The model predicts *likely* text, not *true* text. Usually those coincide;
> when they diverge you get something fluent and wrong with identical
> confidence, because confidence isn't something it computes. You can't
> instruct it away — you change the situation by supplying the text and
> requiring the answer to come from it."

**"How do you make citations trustworthy?"**
> "Don't let the model generate them. We write the filename and page — read from
> database rows — into the evidence text before the model sees it, so it copies
> a label instead of recalling a fact. The residual risk is attaching the wrong
> block's label to the right sentence, which post-generation verification would
> catch."

**"How would you evaluate this?"**
> "Honestly, today I couldn't — our parameters are reasoned, not measured, and
> the fusion constant is the value from the paper rather than tuned on our data.
> I'd build a golden set: fifty questions over ten real documents with expected
> answers and source pages, including ten the documents can't answer so refusal
> is tested. Then recall@30 and precision@5 for retrieval, citation accuracy and
> refusal correctness for generation. First experiment would be chunk size,
> because it has the widest downstream effect and changing it later means
> re-embedding."

*This answer scores higher than a confident false one. Say it exactly like
that.*

## F.6 System design

**"Design a document Q&A system."** — you have built one. Use Chapter 02's nine
steps out loud: requirements → non-functional targets → constraints →
back-of-envelope numbers → data model → components → interfaces → bottlenecks
and failure modes → decisions with alternatives.

**Do the arithmetic aloud.** "A 100-page document is about 200 chunks at 6 KB
each, so 1.2 MB; a thousand users with twenty documents is 24 GB, which fits one
Postgres instance — so no separate vector database." **That single move
separates candidates.**

**"How would you scale it to a million users?"**
> "I'd find what binds first rather than adding servers. Here it's database
> connections — fifteen for the project, five per API instance, so three
> instances starve the worker. First move is transaction-mode pooling, then more
> API instances since they're stateless.
>
> Then the model API's rate limit, which key rotation stretches but doesn't
> remove — that's where request queueing and model routing come in. Then worker
> throughput, which scales horizontally. Postgres would eventually need read
> replicas, and vectors might move to a dedicated store — the seam exists."

**"Where would this break first in production?"** → connection budget, then
model rate limits, then worker capacity. Then volunteer: "and operationally, the
first thing to break my sleep would be no backups."

## F.7 Deployment and production

Use Part Q of Chapter 17 — the two-minute deployment answer — plus:

**"Liveness vs readiness?"** → "Alive, so restart it" vs "can serve traffic now,
so stop routing but don't restart" — and why restarting a not-ready process
turns a blip into a loop.

**"How do you debug a production failure?"** → symptom precisely → is it
everything or one thing → what changed → signals in order (ps, health, logs by
correlation id, queue depth, `pg_stat_activity`) → one hypothesis → *then* code.

## F.8 Security

**"Authentication vs authorisation?"** → identity vs permission; and "broken
authorisation is quieter and lasts longer, which is why our four tenancy leaks
survived — every endpoint returned results and nobody was denied anything."

**"Why hash passwords rather than encrypt?"** → reversible needs a key that
leaks with the database, and you never need the original back. Then salt, then
key stretching, then bcrypt.

**"Explain CSRF."** → another site causes your browser to send an authenticated
request because cookies are attached automatically; attacker can't read the
reply, so it's about causing actions. Defences: `SameSite=strict` and a
double-submit token the attacker's script can't read.

**"Prompt injection?"** → same shape as SQL injection, no structural fix
available, so bound the damage: no tools, arithmetic in Python, labels written by
code.

# Part G — Engineering Communication

Six habits. Practise them; they are worth more than another fact.

**1. Think aloud.** Silence is unreadable. "Let me think about the failure modes
first — what happens if the worker dies mid-job?" tells the interviewer how you
reason, which is what they are buying.

**2. Say "I don't know" properly.** The formula is *boundary → adjacent
knowledge → how I'd find out.*
> "I haven't used Kafka. What I know is our Redis broker isn't durable — jobs
> are lost if it dies — and I understand Kafka keeps an ordered log with
> consumer offsets so consumers can replay. I'd read the delivery guarantees
> first, because that's the property that changes the design."

**Never bluff.** Interviewers ask follow-ups precisely to test the edge of your
knowledge, and a bluff collapses on question two.

**3. Give trade-offs unprompted.** Every "we chose X" should carry "which costs
us Y". It is the fastest signal of seniority available to you.

**4. Handle disagreement without folding or fighting.**
> "That's a fair point — say more about the case you're thinking of?" … "I see
> it: with several instances, my start-command migration would delay every
> instance. I'd move it to a pre-deploy step at that point."

**Changing your mind on evidence is a strength. Changing it on tone is not.**
If you still disagree: "I think X because [reason] — but I'd want to measure
before committing."

**5. Draw, do not describe.** For any architecture question, draw boxes and
arrows and *say what crosses each arrow*: browser → API → Postgres, API → Redis
→ worker, API → model. Then label the failure modes on the arrows. Five boxes is
enough.

**6. Explain failures without hedging.** "The retrieval cache was never
invalidated on delete" — not "there may have been a slight issue with the
caching layer". **Precision reads as competence; vagueness reads as
concealment.**

**Weak:** "I'm a hard worker and a fast learner." **Strong:** evidence, then
